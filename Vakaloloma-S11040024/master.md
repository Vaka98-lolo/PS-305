# PS 305 Health Psychology - “Problem-Focused vs Emotion-Focused Coping: A Comparative Analysis of Stress and Coping Strategies” 
## Reading 1:    
Hart, Wearing, and Headey (1995) examined psychological well-being among police officers using a Perceived Quality of Life (PQOL) framework. The study involved 527 participants and applied a quantitative approach using structural equation modelling to analyse questionnaire data. The findings revealed that both positive and negative work experiences significantly influenced well-being, with organisational factors being more impactful than operational factors. Additionally, personality traits such as neuroticism and extraversion were strong predictors of psychological well-being. Coping strategies also played a key role, with problem-focused coping linked to positive work experiences, while emotion-focused coping was associated with negative outcomes. The study concluded that coping strategies significantly influence psychological well-being, highlighting the effectiveness of problem-focused coping. 

## Reading 2: 
Park and Adler (2003) investigated how coping styles predict physical health and psychological well-being among first-year medical students. The study involved 71 students and used a longitudinal research design, assessing participants at the beginning and end of the academic year. The findings showed that both physical health and psychological well-being declined over time due to academic stress. However, students who used higher levels of problem-focused coping and approach-based emotion-focused coping experienced less deterioration in physical health. Psychological well-being was more strongly related to baseline functioning than coping style. The study concluded that coping strategies play an important role in predicting physical health outcomes, showing that both problem-focused coping and certain types of emotion-focused coping can be beneficial. 

## Reading 3:
Aljaffer et al. (2025) examined the relationship between coping strategies, stress, and anxiety among medical students at King Saud University. The study involved 332 participants and used a quantitative cross-sectional design with a structured questionnaire to measure stress, anxiety, and coping strategies. The findings showed that most students experienced moderate to high levels of stress and anxiety, with academic stressors being the most common. Problem-focused coping strategies such as planning and seeking social support were more frequently used than emotion-focused coping strategies. Additionally, coping strategies were negatively associated with anxiety, indicating their role in reducing stress. The study concluded that coping strategies are important in managing stress and improving student well-being. This study is relevant as it highlights the effectiveness of problem-focused coping in reducing anxiety and supporting psychological well-being.

##Reading 4:

Folkman and Lazarus (1980) examined how individuals cope with stressful events in daily life using a cognitive-phenomenological framework of stress. The study involved 100 middle-aged adults who reported their coping responses to stressful situations over one year through interviews and questionnaires. The findings showed that both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping were used in the majority of situations, with individuals often using both strategies together. Coping patterns varied depending on the context and how the situation was appraised, with problem-focused coping used when situations were controllable and emotion-focused coping used when situations had to be accepted. The study concluded that coping is a flexible and dynamic process influenced by situational factors. This study is relevant as it demonstrates that both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping play important roles depending on the nature of the stressor.

**Reading 5**

Carver, Scheier, and Weintraub (1989) examined different coping strategies by developing a multidimensional coping inventory known as the COPE scale. The study involved participants across multiple stages, including undergraduate students, and used a quantitative approach with questionnaires to assess coping responses to stress. The findings identified several distinct coping strategies, including problem-focused coping, emotion-focused coping, and less effective coping responses such as behavioral disengagement. The results showed that individuals use a combination of coping strategies depending on both situational and personal factors. The study concluded that coping is a multidimensional and flexible process that cannot be explained by a single strategy. This study is relevant as it highlights the importance of both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping in managing stress.

**##Critique and Comparison of Coping Strategies:**

Research on coping strategies consistently highlights the importance of both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping, although their effectiveness varies across contexts. Studies such as Hart et al. (1995) and Aljaffer et al. (2025) strongly support the effectiveness of problem-focused coping, showing that it is associated with positive psychological outcomes and reduced stress levels. These findings suggest that actively addressing stressors through planning, problem-solving, and seeking support is generally more beneficial than focusing solely on emotional regulation. However, Park and Adler (2003) provide a more balanced perspective, demonstrating that both problem-focused coping and certain forms of emotion-focused coping can contribute to better physical health outcomes. Similarly, Folkman and Lazarus (1980) argue that coping is situational, with problem-focused coping being more effective in controllable situations, while emotion-focused coping is more appropriate when stressors cannot be changed. Burke et al. (2005) further explain that psychological stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) system, leading to the release of cortisol, a hormone associated with stress regulation. Their findings showed that prolonged stress and impaired cortisol recovery were more common among depressed individuals, highlighting the importance of effective coping strategies in maintaining both psychological and physiological well-being.

Despite these valuable insights, the studies also have several limitations that must be considered. Many of the studies rely on self-report questionnaires, which may introduce bias due to inaccurate recall or socially desirable responses. Additionally, the samples used in the studies vary significantly, ranging from police officers (Hart et al., 1995) to medical students (Park & Adler, 2003; Aljaffer et al., 2025), which may limit the generalisability of the findings across different populations. Cultural and contextual differences may also influence coping behaviours, yet these factors are not always fully explored. Furthermore, Carver et al. (1989) highlight that coping is a multidimensional process, suggesting that categorising coping strictly into problem-focused and emotion-focused strategies may oversimplify the complexity of human responses to stress. Burke et al. (2005) also noted that cortisol responses may differ according to age, severity of depression, and situational factors, indicating that stress responses are highly complex and influenced by both psychological and biological processes.

Overall, the evidence suggests that problem-focused coping is generally more effective in managing stress, particularly in situations where individuals have control over the stressor. However, emotion-focused coping remains important, especially in situations that require acceptance or emotional regulation. Therefore, coping should be understood as a flexible and dynamic process in which individuals use different strategies depending on the context. This integrated perspective provides a more comprehensive understanding of how individuals manage stress and maintain psychological well-being.

**Introduction**

Stress is a common experience that affects individuals in academic, occupational, and personal settings. The way people respond to stress can significantly influence their psychological well-being, physical health, and overall quality of life. Coping strategies refer to the cognitive and behavioural efforts individuals use to manage stressful situations and emotional challenges. Among the most widely discussed coping strategies are problem-focused coping and emotion-focused coping. Problem-focused coping aims to directly address the source of stress through actions such as planning, seeking information, and problem-solving, whereas emotion-focused coping attempts to regulate emotional responses to stress through acceptance, emotional support, or avoidance. Stress also affects physiological functioning through the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) system, which releases cortisol, a hormone associated with stress regulation (Burke et al., 2005). Prolonged stress and abnormal cortisol responses may negatively affect both mental and physical health. Although both coping strategies are commonly used, researchers continue to debate which strategy is more effective in promoting psychological well-being. This paper argues that problem-focused coping is generally more effective in reducing stress and improving well-being; however, emotion-focused coping also plays an important role in situations where stressors cannot be directly controlled. Through the analysis of empirical studies, this paper demonstrates that coping is a flexible and context-dependent process (Burke et al., 2005).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 

**Discussion of Coping Strategies**

Research consistently shows that problem-focused coping contributes positively to psychological well-being and stress management. Hart, Wearing, and Headey (1995) examined psychological well-being among police officers using a Perceived Quality of Life (PQOL) framework. Their study involved 527 participants and used structural equation modelling to analyse questionnaire data. The findings revealed that problem-focused coping was associated with positive work experiences and improved psychological well-being, whereas emotion-focused coping was linked to negative outcomes. These results suggest that actively addressing stressors allows individuals to gain greater control over stressful situations, leading to more positive psychological outcomes (Hart et al., 1995).

Similarly, Aljaffer et al. (2025) investigated the relationship between coping strategies, stress, and anxiety among medical students at King Saud University. Using a quantitative cross-sectional design involving 332 participants, the researchers found that problem-focused coping strategies such as planning and seeking social support were more frequently used than emotion-focused coping strategies. Additionally, coping strategies were negatively associated with anxiety, indicating their effectiveness in reducing stress levels. Academic stressors were identified as the most common source of stress among students. These findings further support the argument that problem-focused coping strategies are highly effective in managing stress and improving well-being in demanding environments (Aljaffer et al., 2025).

Stress also affects physiological functioning through the activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) system, which releases cortisol, a hormone associated with stress regulation. Burke, Davis, Otte, and Mohr (2005) conducted a meta-analysis involving 196 participants, including clinically depressed and non-depressed individuals, to examine cortisol responses to psychological stress. Their findings revealed that depressed individuals experienced impaired stress recovery and abnormal cortisol response patterns following psychological stressors. The researchers further explained that prolonged stress and impaired cortisol recovery may negatively affect both psychological and physical health. These findings highlight the importance of effective coping strategies in stress management and psychological well-being (Burke et al., 2005).

However, the effectiveness of coping strategies is not always straightforward. Park and Adler (2003) examined how coping styles predicted physical health and psychological well-being among first-year medical students. Their longitudinal study involving 71 students found that both physical health and psychological well-being declined over the academic year due to stress. Nevertheless, students who used higher levels of problem-focused coping and approach-based emotion-focused coping experienced less deterioration in physical health. Unlike Hart et al. (1995), this study suggests that some forms of emotion-focused coping may also be beneficial, particularly when combined with active coping strategies. Therefore, coping effectiveness may depend on the nature of the stressor and the coping method used (Park & Adler, 2003).

Folkman and Lazarus (1980) provide additional support for the situational nature of coping. Their study examined how 100 middle-aged adults coped with stressful life events over one year using interviews and questionnaires. The findings revealed that both problem-focused and emotion-focused coping were used in most stressful situations, often simultaneously. Problem-focused coping was more commonly used when individuals believed the situation could be changed, whereas emotion-focused coping was used when stressors needed to be accepted. This study demonstrates that coping is a flexible and dynamic process influenced by how individuals perceive stressful events (Folkman & Lazarus, 1980).

Furthermore, Carver, Scheier, and Weintraub (1989) expanded the understanding of coping by developing the multidimensional COPE inventory. Their study identified several distinct coping strategies, including problem-focused coping, emotion-focused coping, and less effective coping responses such as behavioural disengagement. The findings demonstrated that individuals often use multiple coping strategies depending on personal and situational factors. This challenges the assumption that one coping strategy is universally superior and highlights the complexity of human coping behaviour (Carver et al., 1989).

**Critique and Evaluation**

Although the reviewed studies provide valuable insights into coping strategies, several limitations should be considered. One major limitation is the reliance on self-report questionnaires, which may introduce bias due to inaccurate memory or socially desirable responses. Participants may underreport ineffective coping strategies or exaggerate positive behaviours. As a result, the accuracy of the findings may be affected. Additionally, Burke et al. (2005) noted that stress responses such as cortisol activity may vary according to factors including age, severity of depression, and situational context, suggesting that psychological stress responses are highly complex and difficult to measure accurately (Burke et al., 2005).

Another limitation concerns the generalisability of the studies. Many of the studies focused on specific populations such as police officers and medical students. These groups experience unique forms of stress that may differ from those faced by the general population. Consequently, the findings may not apply equally to all individuals or cultural contexts. Cultural differences may also influence how individuals perceive stress and choose coping strategies, yet these factors were not fully explored in the studies reviewed.

Additionally, categorising coping strategies strictly into problem-focused and emotion-focused coping may oversimplify the complexity of human behaviour. Carver et al. (1989) argue that coping is multidimensional, involving a variety of behavioural and emotional responses that interact depending on the situation. Similarly, Folkman and Lazarus (1980) demonstrated that individuals often use both coping strategies together rather than separately. Therefore, understanding coping requires a more flexible perspective that recognises the interaction between personal, situational, environmental, and physiological factors.

Despite these limitations, the overall findings consistently suggest that problem-focused coping is generally more effective in situations where individuals can directly influence the stressor. At the same time, emotion-focused coping remains valuable in helping individuals regulate emotions during uncontrollable situations. Therefore, an integrated approach that combines both coping strategies provides a more comprehensive understanding of stress management and psychological well-being.

**Conclusion**

In conclusion, coping strategies play a critical role in determining how individuals respond to stress and maintain psychological well-being. The studies reviewed in this paper demonstrate that problem-focused coping is generally more effective in reducing stress and promoting positive outcomes, particularly when individuals have control over stressful situations. However, emotion-focused coping also serves an important function in helping individuals manage emotional distress when stressors cannot be changed. The evidence suggests that coping is not a fixed process but rather a flexible and context-dependent response to stress. Understanding the strengths and limitations of different coping strategies can help individuals develop healthier ways of managing stress and improving their overall well-being.
